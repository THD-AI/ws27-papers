# Section 4 plan – lay reader

Reader: a retired shopkeeper with no science, who reads the long weekend features and likes a picture that explains something. I read sections 3 and 4 first, then the plan (sections 3, 0 and 6 of `VISUALS-PURPOSE.md`). Nothing is drawn yet; this is what I picture from the words.

## Scores

| Picture | "I'd get it" |
|---|---|
| The slabs and gauges | 6 / 10 |
| The film on the grid | 4 / 10 |

## 1. What the slab picture would teach me

Three slabs of floor in a row, with a little meter across each of the two cracks between them. Both meters move. The question is which slab sank. Two small pictures answer: maybe the middle one went down, or maybe the two outer ones did. The meters looked the same either way. So you can't tell from the meters, and someone has to guess.

That part I'd get, and I like that it's a building, not a diagram. But I'm not sure I'd get it in five seconds, for two reasons:

- **If the slabs are drawn sunk, I can see which one sank.** Then the whole point falls over: I'm not guessing, I'm looking. In the main picture the slabs need to be hidden – under a floor, behind a wall, greyed out – so that only the meters show. The two small "stories" should look like guesses (dotted, with a question mark), not like the answer.
- **The two stories look different to my eye.** Middle down makes a V; ends down makes a hump. If a gauge shows which way things moved (and I'd guess a real builder's gauge does), the two stories would not give the same readings at all. The picture has to make clear that the gauge only says "moved" or "didn't move", nothing about up or down or how far. Otherwise a reader with a handy relative in the building trade will say it's wrong.

## 2. The jump from slabs to the grid

This is where I'd be lost. I go from three slabs in a row to a square of small dots, three frames of it, and no labels. Nothing tells me that a dot is a slab and a lit mark is a gauge. The plan says the same lit mark will be reused so I "see the building turn into the grid", but a matching dot colour alone won't carry me there. I'd need one plain sentence or a tiny key: "The real chip is a square of these, not a row. Dots are the hidden parts, the lit marks are the gauges."

Frame 3 going dark again would also puzzle me. If the slab has sunk and nobody fixed it, why does the gauge go quiet? The text says a light means "changed since the round before", but a real gauge keeps showing what it showed. In the slab picture, the engineer would need to reset the gauge or mark a new line after every reading. Say that in the slab scene, and the dark frame 3 makes sense in the film.

"Three frames" also won't say "film" to me unless something says the frames come one after another, very fast. One line under them – "one look every millionth of a second" – would do it.

## 3. What I'd need for "two stories, you can't tell which"

- The same lit gauges shown twice, identical, side by side, one with each story underneath. My eye has to check that the readings really are the same.
- The slabs or qubits themselves hidden or faint in the main picture, so I'm reading only the lights.
- One short reason it matters: guess wrong and the stored answer is wrong. The caption ending "so a program has to guess which it was, before the next frame" is good; I'd add why a wrong guess hurts, in a few words.
- On the grid, the two stories drawn the same way as on the slabs (one dot in the middle against two at the corners), so I recognise the pattern from the first picture.

## 4. Words that would trip me

- **Gauge, settlement gauge, tell-tale:** I'd half-know "gauge" (like a pressure gauge with a needle). "Tell-tale" and "settlement" mean nothing to me. If the reader sees a word, use "crack meter" or just show it.
- **Slab:** fine if it's drawn. "Floor slab" is clear enough.
- **In section, section view:** to me "section" means part of the newspaper or section 4 of this article. Say "cut through" or "seen from the side".
- **Joint:** I'd think of a knee. "The gap between two slabs" is plainer.
- **Frames:** fine if they look like film frames; meaningless if they're three squares of dots.
- **z0, z1, qubit 4, 2 and 6:** the plan keeps these off the picture. Good; keep it that way.

## 5. One-line fixes

1. Hide the slabs in the main picture and show only the meters; draw the two stories as dotted guesses with a question mark, never as the answer.
2. Make the gauge a plain "moved / not moved" light that the engineer resets after each look, so the stories match and frame 3 going dark makes sense.
3. Put one line or a tiny key between the two pictures: "The real chip is a square, not a row: dots are the hidden parts, lights are the gauges, one look every millionth of a second."
