The logic is good and very close to how we actually think: a tell-tale across a joint reads *relative* movement of the two sides and nothing else. That is exactly a parity check. But the plan names the wrong instrument, puts it on the wrong surface, and its "two stories" would be told apart by any real gauge, because real gauges show direction. All four are fixable without changing the idea.

## 1. The device and where it sits

**"Settlement gauge" is the wrong word, and it matters.** In the trade a settlement gauge (a levelling stud, settlement plate or settlement cell) measures how far *one point* has moved against a fixed datum, usually by levelling back to a benchmark. That is reading the slab directly – the opposite of what the analogy needs. Drop the word.

**The right device is a tell-tale crack monitor** (the common make is a two-plate acrylic monitor). Two overlapping plastic strips, palm-sized: one carries a printed millimetre grid, the other is clear with a red hairline cross. Each strip is screwed or glued to one side of the crack or joint, so the cross sits at the zero of the grid on the day it is fixed. When one side moves against the other, the cross moves across the grid: sideways for opening or closing, up or down for one side dropping against the other. It is read by eye to about half a millimetre and written in a log with the date. This is the device to draw.

A **joint meter** (vibrating-wire or three-axis, used on dam and tunnel joints) does the same job electronically. It is truer to "read at fixed intervals by a machine", but no lay reader knows it and it draws as a featureless cylinder. Not for the picture.

**Where it sits:** tell-tales go on vertical faces – a wall or a facade – across a crack or across a movement joint between two parts of a building, for example where an extension meets the original house. They are not put on floors (they would be walked on and kicked off). So "three floor slabs seen in section, with a gauge across each joint" is off twice: a gauge on a floor is wrong, and a section cuts through the structure, so a monitor fixed on a face cannot honestly be shown in it. Draw an **elevation** (the face as you would see it standing in front), with the tell-tale on the face across each joint.

**How to make it recognisable:** almost no lay reader has seen one, so the drawing has to teach it once. Recommended: in step 0, one gauge drawn large beside the row (grid, cross at the centre, two screws either side of the joint); the gauges on the row are small copies of it. "Moved" is the cross off-centre on the grid. People who own an old house, or have read a surveyor's report, will know it at once; everyone else gets it from the inset in a second, because a cross that has left its target is self-explanatory.

## 2. The claims, one by one

| Claim | Holds in the trade? |
|---|---|
| A unit that sinks moves both gauges on its joints | Yes. |
| An end unit has one joint and moves one gauge | Yes, if the end is a free edge. If the end of a floor slab meets a perimeter wall, there is a joint (and a gauge) there too. Use units with nothing beyond the ends. |
| Two neighbouring units that sink together leave the gauge between them unchanged | Yes. This is exactly why tell-tales miss uniform settlement, and why we also level. |
| "The middle sank" and "both ends sank" move the same two gauges | **No, not on a real gauge.** See below. |
| A gauge shows a change since the last reading | **Not quite.** A tell-tale shows movement since the day it was fixed. The *engineer* compares the reading with the last line in the log. |
| A misreading happens once, then the reading is right again | Yes. Parallax or a wrong figure in the log gives a one-off spike, and we discount a single outlier against the readings either side. True to life. |

**The direction problem (the one a builder will object to first).** If the middle unit drops by 2 mm, the left gauge reads "right side down 2" and the right gauge reads "left side down 2". If both end units drop by 2 mm, the left gauge reads "left side down 2" and the right gauge "right side down 2". The crosses move the opposite way. Any engineer reading those two gauges tells the stories apart at once, so as drawn the picture says the opposite of what the article needs.

**The fix is better than the original, and fully true:** make the second story **"both end units rose"** (heave: clay swelling after a wet season, or frost) instead of "both end units sank". Middle down 2 mm and both ends up 2 mm give identical readings on both gauges, sign and all. This is a real limit of the trade, said in every site meeting: *a tell-tale tells you the two sides moved against each other; it cannot tell you which side moved.* That is the sentence the figure is for.

It also lines up with the code exactly. The two stories differ by "all three units moved the same amount", which no gauge on the row can see – the article's silent line in section 5. One honest warning for the caption or the text: in a building, uniform settlement is harmless (we shrug at it); in the code, the matching move is the logical error. The analogy shows why it is *invisible*; it must not suggest it is *harmless*.

If heave is judged too unfamiliar, the fallback is to show "moved" only as the accent dot and never move the drawn cross. That is weaker: a builder sees an instrument whose reading is hidden.

**Other things a builder would notice, none fatal:**
- Real units do not drop independently and stay level. A suspended floor slab does not sink at all – its supports do. Ground-bearing slabs can settle (badly compacted fill), but their joints are usually dowelled precisely to stop one side dropping against the other. Use units that are meant to move separately (below).
- A settling unit usually tilts rather than dropping flat, so its two gauges would read different amounts. Fine for the picture: draw it dropping flat, slightly.
- Real readings also wander with the seasons (thermal and moisture movement, a millimetre or so). That is the trade's version of noisy checks; no need to draw it.
- Two settlements of the same unit add up; two flips of a qubit cancel. Do not draw a unit sinking twice.

## 3. A more natural setting with the same logic

- **Best: a row of three structurally separate blocks with movement joints between them, in elevation** – a short terrace, or one long low building split into three sections on separate foundations. Splitting a building on poor ground into blocks with full-height settlement joints is standard practice, and the tell-tale across the joint on the facade is the textbook use of the device. It reads at once: three boxes, two vertical gaps, a small gauge across each gap. To stay distinct from the Renovate house, draw them as flat-roofed blocks, no pitched roof, no windows or few, no people.
- **Foundation pads under a row of columns:** the engineering is right (differential settlement between neighbouring columns is what we design against), but there is no joint to put a gauge on, and the reading is done by levelling each column – direct reading again. Reject.
- **Precast panels:** they hang from the frame and do not settle by themselves. Reject.
- **Bridge deck with expansion joints:** the joints sit over the piers, so when a pier settles both span ends at that joint drop together and the joint barely moves; the spans tilt instead. The mapping breaks. Reject.

## Required changes

1. Replace "settlement gauge" with "tell-tale" (crack monitor) everywhere; a settlement gauge reads one point against a datum.
2. Change the second story from "both end slabs sank" to "both end blocks rose" (heave), so both gauges read identically, sign included; or else never move the drawn cross.
3. Change "three floor slabs in section" to "three separate blocks with movement joints between them, in elevation"; tell-tales go on vertical faces, not floors.
4. Change "a gauge shows a change since the last reading" to "the engineer notes whether it changed since the last reading"; the gauge itself shows movement since it was fixed.
5. Add one large tell-tale (grid, cross at zero, screws either side of the joint) beside the row in step 0, so a lay reader learns the device once.
6. Keep the end units free-ended (nothing beyond them), so an end unit truly has one joint.
7. Do not let the figure suggest uniform movement is harmless: invisible to every gauge, yes; harmless in the code, no.

### Is the glass tell-tale true to the trade

Yes. It is a real and old device, still fitted on historic masonry (in French a *témoin à lame de verre*; the plaster-dab version is the *témoin en plâtre*, in German *Gipsmarke*). It is crude and has largely been replaced by grid monitors wherever numbers are needed, but for "has it moved, yes or no" it is the honest picture. Three corrections to how the plan describes it:

- **How it is fixed.** Not "glued across the joint". A strip of glass (often about microscope-slide size, or a longer strip) is bedded on a dab of mortar, plaster or epoxy at *each end only*, one end on each side of the crack or joint, with the middle spanning the gap unbonded. That is what makes it work: the two ends are carried by the two sides, and the glass between them takes the movement. It is usually dated in pencil or scratched on the wall beside it. The drawing must show the two end dabs; they also make it read as a fitted instrument, not a stuck-on label.
- **What cracks it.** One side dropping against the other (shear) cracks it readily, and so does the joint opening. Closing tends to debond or crush the ends rather than crack the glass cleanly. The picture only uses a block dropping, so "cracks if the blocks either side move against each other" holds for everything shown. Very small movements (a fraction of a millimetre) do not crack it; fine for an analogy where a block "drops by one step".
- **"A fresh strip after every look."** Not practice. A whole strip stays on the wall for years; at each inspection a cracked strip is recorded, photographed and replaced with a new dated one, often fixed beside the old one. The result the plan needs is the same: if every cracked strip is renewed at each look, a cracked strip means "moved since the last look". Say it that way: "at each look, any cracked strip is noted and replaced with a new one". A builder accepts that; "a fresh strip after every look" would make him smile.

**Recognisable once the key is drawn:** yes, more than any grid monitor. Everyone knows what cracked glass means, and the key sentence "a glass strip: it cracks if the blocks either side move" carries it. Draw the crack as one clean diagonal line through the strip at the joint, the way a shear crack runs, not as a shattered star (that reads as a blow, which is the knock case the plan dropped).

### What is still wrong for a builder

1. **"Movement joint" is the wrong joint for a glass strip.** A movement (expansion) joint is designed to open and close with the seasons; a glass strip across it would crack every summer from nothing. Builders put tell-tales across *settlement* joints, or across the crack where an extension meets the old house. In the plan, write "settlement joint" (blocks on separate foundations); on the page, do not name the joint at all, just "where two blocks meet".
2. **Ground line.** A block that "drops by one step" in elevation needs a ground line, or the reader sees a block moving down a white page rather than settling. Keep one ground line under all three; the dropped block's base goes below it by one step. In the silent case (all three dropped together) the ground line is the only thing that shows they moved at all.
3. **The silent-case label.** "The dangerous case: nothing shows" is right for the code, but a builder reads uniform settlement as the harmless case. Tie the danger to whoever reads the strips, e.g. "nothing cracks, so nobody can tell" or "the case no strip can show". The danger then comes from the article's text, not from the building.
4. **The dropped block keeps its height.** Draw it the same size, simply moved down, so the step shows at both its roof and its base. A block that looks shorter reads as a different building.
