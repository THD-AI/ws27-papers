## The short version

On 5 October 2026 Deutsche Telekom told investors that software agents now do a large part of customer service at T-Mobile, its American subsidiary, and that it wants the same in Europe by 2030. {{R6-01 R6-03}} I wanted to know whether that can be copied.

My answer is that it can't be bought and bolted onto a company. It depends on two buildings: the company's own systems, and the country the company works in.

:::scene walls

@step 0
My father, a civil engineer, gave me the picture for both. In America 94% of new family homes have timber frames: a new cable is pulled down through the hollow wall behind the board. In Germany about two in three residential buildings approved in 2020 were brick or other masonry: a new cable runs in a channel cut into the wall and plastered over. A brick house is solid and built to stand. But once it needs new pipes, cables or a network, the work is heavy and slow. {{NAHB-2026 DESTATIS-2021 BAUNETZ-DIN18015}}

@step 1 screen
Take one new cable to three walls: a timber frame, and the same plastered brick wall twice.

@step 2 screen
In the timber frame the cable drops from above into one hollow bay, and nothing shows. In brick it can be screwed onto the surface in a duct and painted over: quick, and the wall behind it stays as it was. In this article that's **repainting**. Or a channel is cut, the cable laid and the wall plastered again: slow, and the cable becomes part of the wall. That's **renovating**.

@caption The same new cable in three walls. In a timber frame it's dropped from above into one hollow bay. In plastered brick it follows the installation zones: across under the ceiling, then down beside the corner. It can be screwed onto the surface in a duct and painted over: a proper installation, quick, and the wall behind it stays as it was. In this article that stands for repainting: the new part sits on top, and nothing behind it changes. Or a channel is cut, the cable laid and the wall plastered again: slow, and the cable becomes part of the wall. That's renovating.
:::

Here the walls are the conditions a firm works under, and the cables are the connections between its systems. American walls open fast: a firm can hire and let people go quickly, check the results afterwards, and buy its software from next door. German walls protect the people inside and are built to last; the plans are checked before work starts, and every opening is cut and plastered again. Political economists have names for the two kinds: liberal and coordinated market economies, with the United States and Germany as the standard examples. {{HS-2001}} The houses are only a picture of that difference. They prove nothing about software.

Inside its timber walls, T-Mobile's wiring was as tangled as anyone's, and it rewired while its agents went in. Telekom's wiring in Europe is a row of old houses, each wired differently, and Telekom says so itself. The research on how firms adopt new technology sorts what speeds or slows them into three places: the technology, the organisation and the environment. {{TOE-1990}} In Germany each has its weight: know-how that doesn't reach the firms, laws that inspect before the wall is opened, and technology bought from America. And the staff stay, so the agents have to help them rather than replace them.

In short, the order I'd follow: measure first, agree the rules with the works council, keep the choice of supplier open, train the staff, build one customer record, then hand the agent its keys one stage at a time, each tested first. The last section explains each step.

## 1. What an AI system is

:::scene terms

@caption Four everyday objects for four parts of an AI system. The first is what most people picture as AI. A working system needs the other three.

@step 0
The picture most people carry is one mind that understands everything and does what it's told. What a company actually builds looks more like the equipment of a building site.

@step 1
The language model is the handbook: it answers fluently from what it once read, and sometimes wrongly. Retrieval is the plans archive: the system looks up the company's own documents first, so the answer is only as good as the archive. An agent is the handbook given keys and a job. It reads the job, does a step, looks at what happened and decides the next step, in a loop, until the job is done or it hands over to a person. It may act, not only answer, but only where its keys fit. And the cables connect it to the billing system, the customer record and the network.

:::

The handbook can be bought, but it has to be tested on the company's own cases before it gets any keys. The version in use is fixed, and the tests run again whenever the model, its instructions, the documents or the connections change. Everything else the company builds itself, and that's most of the work.

## 2. The American result

@where United States · T-Mobile US
T-Mobile's figures, shown at Deutsche Telekom's investor day and in its filings with the American securities regulator, are the company's own and aren't audited. They're still the best public evidence of what agents do in a large customer service. {{R6-02 R6-06 R6-09}}

:::scene numbers

@caption What the two headline figures count. Of 100 contacts, the release speaks of 40 without saying whose, and T-Mobile's own slide counts 32 that start with the software. Then contacts per customer account in a year, at the two dates that are public.

@step 0
**The 40%.** Deutsche Telekom's release says AI agents "now handle 40 percent of customer contacts" in the United States. I couldn't find that figure in T-Mobile's own material, and it matters, because this is the result Telekom holds up for Europe. {{R6-01 R6-06}}

@step 1
T-Mobile's slide gives 32%, and says what it counts: contacts from contract customers that were "served by an AI agent" in the second quarter of 2026. {{R6-06}}

@step 2
These are contacts where the customer meets the software "from the beginning", in the words of T-Mobile's head of IT. How many of them the software finished isn't published. {{R6-02}}

@step 3
**The 55%.** It comes from two points on T-Mobile's slide: how often an average customer account contacts customer care in a year. At the end of 2021 that was 7.0 times, about once every seven weeks. In mid-2026 it was 3.1 times, about once every four months. {{R6-06 R6-12}}

@step 4
The agent platform was announced in September 2024, almost three years after the first point. Nothing is published in between, so how much of the fall came before the agents isn't known. T-Mobile names other causes too: problems fixed before customers call, and customers doing things in the app. The fall has more causes than the agents. {{R6-08 R6-14}}

:::

So the honest version of the American result is smaller than the headline: about a third of contract customers' contacts start with software, and contacts per account fell by more than half between late 2021 and mid-2026, a period that includes the rewiring. It's still a large change, and it's the one Telekom wants to copy.

Can Germany be measured against it? Not from outside. I put every American figure next to the nearest German one and got ten pairs, and in none of them do the two sides fully count the same thing over the same period. The full table is in the appendix. {{R6-06 R6-16 R6-03}}

## 3. Timber and brick

@where United States · T-Mobile US
**T-Mobile rewired.** Its head of IT told investors that the company had relied on "a largely outsourced, third-party workforce" working on systems owned by suppliers, and replaced it with its own engineers on its own systems. It built one system that holds what the company knows about each customer, and the agents read from it. A customer calling from a T-Mobile phone is recognised automatically, so the agent knows whose contract it may touch. The rebuilding costs money, and not all of it works out: in the third quarter of 2025, a year after the agents were announced, T-Mobile wrote off 278 million dollars on parts of a billing-system replacement it abandoned. On the rebuilt base the agents could do more, and the share of chat conversations that never reached a person rose from about a quarter in mid-2024 to 78% in mid-2026. {{R6-02 R6-06 R6-09}}

@where Germany and Europe · Deutsche Telekom
**Telekom's houses are many, and each is wired differently.** In Germany Telekom already runs Frag Magenta, a chat and phone assistant, for more than 380 kinds of request. After login it can do a few things itself, such as show a bill or change the bank account on a mobile contract; otherwise it hands the customer to a member of staff with a summary. {{R6-16 R6-02}}

Across its European countries, Telekom's own slides say, customers prove who they are in 14 different ways, to be cut to three, and customer data sits in separate systems that still have to be made usable for agents. Above that list the slide states a principle: "Simplify before automating". {{R6-05}}

German firms in general say the same about their wiring. Of those that considered AI but don't use it, 45% named systems that don't fit together and 44% problems with their data. {{R7-06}}

Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson call this pattern the productivity J-curve: like the letter J, the curve dips before it rises. A general-purpose technology pays off only after firms invest in things that don't show up as equipment: new processes, data, skills. While that investment runs, measured productivity lags, and only later does it rise. T-Mobile made that investment as part of building its agents, and its head of IT says it had to come first. Telekom's 14 ways of proving who's calling show that in Europe this work hasn't been done yet. {{BRS-2021}}

## 4. Repainting

:::scene repaint

@caption Two public cases: the saving was announced or taken from the staff first, and taken back later. Klarna froze hiring rather than cutting; at Commonwealth Bank the roles were declared redundant. Sources: Klarna; Information Age on Commonwealth Bank.

@step 0
Repainting is the duct on the surface in figure 1: an agent put in front of the customers while the wiring behind it stays as it was. It's cheaper and faster than renovating, and from the street it looks the same for a while. But because the wiring isn't touched, the saving has to come from somewhere, and it usually comes from the people.

@step 1
Klarna, a Swedish payment company, tried it. In February 2024 it said its assistant did work that, by its own estimate, equalled 700 service agents. By then it had stopped hiring and wasn't replacing staff who left. In May 2025 its chief executive said: "Really, investing in the quality of human support is the way of the future for us", and the company began hiring service staff again. {{R12-14 R12-15}}

Commonwealth Bank of Australia did it faster. In July 2025 it said 45 customer service jobs would go, replaced by a new voice bot. The union disputed the case for it, and in August the bank said its assessment "did not adequately consider all relevant business considerations" and that the roles weren't redundant after all. {{CBA-2025}}

:::

Two cases don't make a rule. But in both, the saving came from the people first and was taken back once it proved wrong: at Klarna over quality, at Commonwealth Bank because, the union said, the work was still there.

## 5. Renovating with the staff at home

T-Mobile did more than rewire. It gave the staff who stayed an assistant of their own, whose recommendations appeared in 74% of their conversations with customers, and where they use it the net promoter score was 12% higher in May 2026. In the one large study I found, of 5,172 support agents, AI help raised the cases solved per hour by 15%, and by about 30% for the least experienced. {{R6-06 R6-09 R4-29}}

:::scene exits

@caption Every full-time position that left Telekom in Germany by an agreed route, 2021 to 2025. One mark is 50 positions. Source: Deutsche Telekom, HR Factbook 2025.

@step 0
An American firm can also let people go while it rewires. T-Mobile booked 531 million dollars of severance by September 2026 under a 2025–2026 restructuring its filings describe as reducing organisational layers; they don't tie it to the agents. Telekom in Germany can't take that route. On 28 May 2026 it agreed with the union ver.di that there will be no dismissals for business reasons until 31 December 2028, for about 60,000 employees under its collective agreements. People still leave by agreed routes: partial retirement, early retirement, severance, civil servants retiring, and transfers to public authorities. From 2021 to 2025, 20,223 full-time positions went that way across Telekom in Germany, about 4,000 a year. {{R6-06 R6-09 R6-11 R11-14 DGB-2026 R12-01}}

@step 1 screen
Each year's column splits into the five agreed routes. Follow one route across the years.

@step 2
Severance is the route that grew: it almost doubled. So Telekom is already getting smaller, by agreement rather than by dismissal, and any saving from agents has to fit into those routes.

:::

:::scene attitudes

@caption Three questions: two asked the same way in both countries, one across the EU. Sources: Ipsos for Google 2025; Ipsos AI Monitor 2025; Eurobarometer 2024 (EU only).

@step 0
So the staff stay while the walls are opened. I think that helps more than it hurts, and the one large study points the same way. The staff aren't against the work either. In a survey that asked both countries the same question, Germans were no more afraid than Americans that AI will take jobs.

@step 1
In another, they trusted their government to regulate it more.

@step 2
One German result stands out: 79% saw digital monitoring of workers negatively, the second-highest share in the European Union. That's the worry any German rollout has to answer first. {{R12-25 R12-28}}

:::

## 6. Know-how: the organisation

:::scene obstacles

@caption Reasons German firms gave in 2025 for not using AI after considering it. Several answers were possible; the six most named are shown. Source: Federal Statistical Office.

@step 0
The first German weight sits in the organisation: people who can do the work. German firms that had considered AI but don't use it were asked why. Most often, 72% of them, they named missing knowledge, ahead of the law. {{R7-06}}

@step 1 screen
The law comes next: unclear legal consequences, 62%, and data protection, 60%. Section 7 returns to both.

:::

A survey by the European Investment Bank asked firms on both sides the same questions about generative AI: about as many European as American firms use it somewhere, 68% against 72%, but fewer use it in two or more parts of the business, 44% against 58%, and fewer in customer service, 22% against 29%. {{R10-01}}

Cohen and Levinthal called the missing piece absorptive capacity, a firm's ability to take in what others know: it can only take in outside knowledge if it already has related knowledge inside. A firm without its own people who understand the wiring can buy the handbook, but it can't install it. That's why I'd train the staff before the tools arrive, not after. {{CL-1990}}

## 7. The law: the environment

The difference in the law is simple to state: in America the checks on a system like this come after launch, in Germany before it. Car approval works the same way. In America the maker certifies a new car's safety itself and the regulator can force a recall later; in the EU an authority approves the type before any car is sold. {{FR-2020-28107 EU-2018-858}}

:::scene permits

@caption One decision, two schedules: in the United States the checks come after launch, in Germany before it. Since 2021 a framework agreement shortens the German route.

@step 0
@where United States · T-Mobile US
At T-Mobile the checks came after. In T-Mobile's public material I found no check by staff before launch, though that doesn't prove there was none. T-Mobile announced its agent platform in September 2024, and about 21 months later a third of contract customers' contacts started with it. A works council in the German sense doesn't exist there; a staff body that T-Mobile had set up itself was ordered dissolved, and a federal appeals court upheld that in January 2024. Other checks come later, as lawsuits and state rules. {{R6-08 R6-06 R11-36 R11-37 R11-41 R11-43}}

@step 1
@where Germany · Deutsche Telekom
At Telekom the works council, elected by the staff, has to be told while the system is still being planned; since 2021 the law names AI expressly. If a system is suited to monitoring staff, the works council has to agree, and a screen that listens to service calls very likely is. Telekom's own privacy and security experts approve a system before it runs, and the data protection regulation requires an impact assessment before risky processing. {{R11-05 R11-07 R11-08 R11-21 R11-22}}

@step 2
Since 2021 Telekom has had a shortcut, the way an approved car type covers every car built to it. Before, its works council's IT committee worked in six-week windows, with more than a hundred cases in each. After a pilot that began in 2018, a group agreement in March 2021 set the rules once: no control of individual performance, evaluations only for groups, logs only for data protection and IT security. A department fills in a profile of its system, the works council checks it, and if nothing goes beyond the rules no separate decision is needed. If the works council refuses a system, a conciliation board decides, and that adds a procedure of its own. A screen that listens to calls probably goes beyond it and needs its own approval, and the agreements that would show how long that takes aren't published. {{R11-12 R11-07 R11-08}}

:::

Under § 87(1) no. 6 of the Works Constitution Act the works council must agree to technical systems suited to monitoring staff. Under the AI Act, customers must be told since 2 August 2026 when they deal with an AI system, and from 2 December 2027 a tool used to monitor or evaluate employees can count as high risk. Firms feel the weight: among those that considered AI but don't use it, 62% named unclear legal consequences and 60% data protection, second and third after missing knowledge. In Germany the inspection comes before the wall is opened, and the work has to be planned for it. {{R11-07 R9-04 R9-05 R7-06}}

## 8. Dependence: the technology

@where Germany and Europe
Half of the larger German firms say they depend on American technology. In a Bitkom survey of 605 German firms with 20 or more staff, published in November 2025, 51% called themselves strongly dependent on the United States for the digital technology they import. {{B-2025}}

@where Germany · Deutsche Telekom
Telekom is better placed than almost any German firm. Since February 2026 it runs an AI factory in Munich with nearly 10,000 graphics processors, which it sells to industrial customers. For its own staff it still uses OpenAI models in its internal assistant and is rolling out 120,000 OpenAI licences, and which model runs Frag Magenta isn't disclosed. If even Telekom reaches for American materials for its own house, firms without a factory of their own will do so all the more. {{R6-24 R6-17 R6-02 R6-05}}

I think that's a problem. A house built from one foreign supplier's parts is at that supplier's mercy: it sets the price, the delivery dates and the rules for what may be stored where. Telekom describes its European platform as able to switch between models. I'd watch that switch more closely than the choice of any single model. {{R6-04}}

## 9. The renovation, in order

What I didn't find in Telekom's public plan is the order, and dates for the work in the walls; Telekom's own target is 2030. The order below isn't copied from T-Mobile, because T-Mobile's sequence can't be shown to have caused its result. It follows from what each step needs from the one before. It's a hypothesis, and the measure in step 1 is how it gets tested.

The frames from the earlier sections each point to a step. The J-curve says the rewiring comes before the return: step 5, and the staged keys of step 6 that build on it. Absorptive capacity says people learn before the tools arrive: step 4. A coordinated economy settles the rules with the staff first: step 2. And the three German weights each have their step: the law in step 2, dependence in step 3, know-how in step 4.

:::steps
1. **Measure first.** One measure for both countries, with its cost: requests finished without a person that didn't come back within a week, set against what the agent costs to build and run. Without it, the 2030 target can't be checked. *Measure:* the pair is published.
2. **Works agreement.** The framework is agreed while the system is planned, not after it's built, with an annex for each new key, so the framework isn't renegotiated every time. The AI Act dates in section 7 set the latest deadlines. *Measure:* signed before the first agent acts. {{R11-07 R9-04 R9-05}}
3. **Choose suppliers.** Keep the switch between model suppliers, and keep sensitive customer data on hosting Telekom controls in Germany. A switch means testing again, so its real length includes the tests and the works council's review. *Measure:* time to switch, tests included. {{R6-04}}
4. **Train staff.** The service staff who stay learn the new tools before the tools reach customers, starting with the least experienced, for whom the measured gain is largest. *Measure:* cases per hour and repeat contacts. {{R4-29}}
5. **One customer record.** Cut the 14 ways of proving who's calling to three and make the customer data usable before agents act on accounts: Telekom's own "simplify before automating", with a date. It's the biggest job on the list: identity, cleaning the data, and connecting systems that were never meant to talk to each other. An agent can't act on an account it can't identify, so everything after this step depends on it. *Measure:* callers recognised without questions. {{R6-05}}
6. **Permissions in stages.** First let the agent read: show a bill, check an order. Then actions that can be undone, such as restarting a line. Changes that fraud could use come last, with a confirmation from the customer and a named person who answers for that kind of action. Before each stage the agent first runs alongside the staff without acting, then has to pass a test set of real past cases, each labelled with the right outcome by experienced staff; afterwards a sample of its conversations is reviewed, and reversed actions and complaints are counted, with a way back to the last stage. The keys are enforced by the systems, not by the model: the agent can only call what its connection allows, however a customer phrases the request. Customers will try to talk it into more, and some messages will carry hidden instructions, so the test set includes those attempts. Tests only make such tricks rarer; what limits the damage when one gets through is that the systems enforce the keys and the account holder confirms risky changes through a second channel, such as the app, not a text message to a number that could itself have been taken over. Every action is logged. Frag Magenta can already change the bank account on a mobile contract after login; that's the key I'd review first. *Measure:* errors per thousand actions, stage by stage. {{R6-16}}
7. **Acceptance.** Before the agent works on its own, the service staff who'll work beside it and the works council sign it off. *Measure:* accepted by both.
8. **Run it.** A named team answers for the agents, nights included, and the cost of each finished request is watched like the electricity bill. What it finds goes back into the measure in step 1. Until the end of 2028 the return has to come from fewer repeat contacts, from staff moving to work software can't do, and from customer service's share of the roughly 4,000 who leave each year by the agreed routes. That's managed attrition and voluntary exits, not repainting: nobody is removed to book the saving, and positions are left unfilled only once the agent has shown it carries the work. *Measure:* cost per finished request. {{R11-14 R12-01}}
:::

What Telekom can copy from T-Mobile, then, isn't the number. It's the work in the walls: one customer record, and callers recognised without questions. The order of the steps is my hypothesis, not T-Mobile's.

## Where this could be wrong

The analogy simplifies. One in five German residential buildings approved in 2020 was already timber, many German firms run modern systems, and software walls, unlike brick, can be rebuilt completely. I use the houses for one property only: how hard it is to add a new connection to what already stands. {{DESTATIS-2021}}

Three findings would show that my argument is wrong. If Telekom reports a German figure built like T-Mobile's 32% before its work on authentication and customer data is finished, the customer record mattered less than I claim. If staff numbers in customer service fall faster than the agreed routes allow, the staff are leaving after all. And if a works agreement for the service systems is published with its dates and shows months of delay, the law is a bigger weight than I give it here.

What I couldn't show from public sources: that the agents alone caused the fall in American service contacts; that any approval delayed a Telekom service system, or that none did; which model runs Frag Magenta and where; and how many of the positions that left, on either side, were in customer service. I spoke to no one at either company; everything here is public.

## How this was made

The question, the argument and the choice of the case are mine. The picture of timber and brick houses is my father's: he's a civil engineer. I used AI assistants to find and open sources (company reports and slides, filings, legal texts, official statistics, published studies), to draft passages from my notes and dictations, and to check every claim against its source. AI critics, each reading as someone else (a professor, an engineer, a practitioner, a reader with no background), went through the drafts, and what they found wrong was fixed. I decided what stays. Every claim carries its source number; on screen, select the mark to open the source.
