What we wanted to know

One recording from a quantum chip, read by two programs, gives two different error counts.

A quantum chip can be excellent and the computer can still fail. The cause sits beside the chip: a program running on an ordinary computer.A cooled quantum chip sends out a strip of film. One reader, the standard program, reads it and gets some answers wrong.

We came across this in a paper Google published in December 2024. It reports the first quantum memory that gets more reliable as it gets bigger, which nobody had managed before. One detail in it is easy to miss. Two programs read the same recording from the same chip and came out with different error rates. A neural network, a program that learns from examples, read it better than the standard program almost every experiment uses.The same film goes to a second reader, a neural network, which gets fewer answers wrong.

So we set ourselves three questions: what the program has to do, why a network can do it better, and at what cost. We answer them in the order we understood them.

1. A qubit doesn't hold still

A qubit's state starts to smear within a thousandth of a blink.

A qubit is the quantum version of a bit, and its weakness is that it doesn't keep its state. It behaves like a pattern drawn on the surface of water: it starts to smear as soon as it's made. On Google's 2024 chip the median qubit lifetime is 85 millionths of a second, about a thousandth of a blink. The operations add errors of their own: each one has a small chance of going wrong.A ripple on water smears as a time line runs to 85 millionths of a second, about a thousandth of a blink.

That sounds harmless until it's set against a real job. A well-known estimate for breaking the encryption that protects much of the internet's traffic puts two numbers side by side. The hardware it assumes slips about once in every thousand basic operations. The calculation it plans needs between two and three billion steps of the costly kind, each built from many basic operations, and a single uncorrected slip can spoil the answer. At one slip per thousand, that means millions of slips where one is enough. Unaided, such hardware cannot finish the calculation.The job is a line of billions of basic operations; about one in every thousand slips.

2. The cure costs what it protects

Ordinary computers protect a bit by storing it three times and taking a vote. That doesn't work for qubits. A quantum state can't be copied, and it can't be looked at to see whether it's still right, because looking destroys it.

The chip never looks at its qubits. It asks questions, and the answers light up.

The way around this is to store the information in the way several qubits relate to each other, and in none of them alone. The simplest version uses three qubits that are only ever all-0 or all-1 together. No single one holds the information, and no copy of it exists anywhere. Many real qubits working together like this are called a logical qubit.Three qubits joined: only ever all 0 or all 1, together.

In the scheme we studied, the surface code, the qubits sit on a square grid, and the size of the grid is called its distance. A bigger grid should protect better, but only if each qubit is good enough. Below a certain error rate, called the threshold, it does. Above it, a bigger grid is worse, since every added qubit is one more thing that smears. For years the hardware sat above it.The smallest grid: nine storing qubits.

3. Asking without looking

Only about half of the qubits store anything. The rest are helpers, each sitting between two or four storing qubits. The smallest grid, distance 3, has 9 storing qubits and 8 helpers, 17 in all. Distance 5 has 49 qubits, and distance 7 has 97.Helpers sit between them: squares ask about flips, diamonds about phase errors.

Once every round, each helper interacts with its neighbours and is measured. One bit comes out. It doesn't say what state any neighbour is in – only whether the number of errors among those neighbours is even or odd. That answer is the same whatever is stored. So measuring it leaves the stored information alone.One helper asks its four neighbours a question; one bit comes back.

These questions are called checks. While nothing goes wrong, a check gives the same answer round after round. When a qubit next to it is hit, the answer changes, and we say the check fires.A flip hits the centre qubit. The chip never sees it, only the two squares beside it light up.

Two kinds of error can hit a qubit. A flip swaps 0 and 1, as in an ordinary bit. A phase error is harder to picture. In the water image, it keeps the pattern's shape and shifts its rhythm: 0 and 1 stay as they are, but "0 plus 1" becomes "0 minus 1". That only shows while the qubit is in both states at once, which an ordinary bit can't be. Half of the checks notice flips and the other half notice phase errors. Some errors are both at once and fire checks of both kinds. We come back to those in section 6.A phase error lights the diamonds instead.

A check can also just report wrongly. Then it fires twice in a row, once when the wrong answer appears and once when the right one returns, although no storing qubit was hit. So a single round is never enough. The checks must be read as a sequence.The grid becomes one frame of a film; in the next frame the same square is lit again: a check that lied.

4. A film of hints

All that leaves a quantum chip is a film of lights, and many different errors make the same lights.

One round of checks takes 1.1 millionths of a second, and a grid of distance 7 has 48 checks. In the time of one blink the chip gets through about ninety thousand rounds and puts out about four million check results, for a single logical qubit.The frames run, one every 1.1 millionths of a second.

While the computer runs, that stream is all that comes out of it. We ended up thinking of it as a film in which a few points light up in every frame.

The fired checks say roughly where something happened and which kind of error it was. They don't say which qubits were hit. Many sets of errors light up the same points.Round 2 again: one flip in the centre explains it, or one flip above and one below.

5. The decoder, and the one mistake that matters

[figure line: not built yet]

A program on a classical computer watches that film and decides which errors happened. It's called the decoder. Nothing on the chip gets repaired: the decoder keeps a record of the errors it believes in, and the final result is corrected in software. For simply storing a qubit that's enough. For calculating it isn't, because some operations can only go ahead once the decoder has answered. So its speed matters too (section 8).

The decoder guesses: from the checks that fired, it picks one explanation. Most small mistakes in that guess are harmless. A wrong guess that fires the same checks usually differs from the truth in a way the code doesn't care about.

The harmful case looks like this. A correction acts on the grid just as errors do. If the real errors and the decoder's correction together make an unbroken line across the grid, from one edge to the opposite edge, the stored value has changed. No check fires, because every check along such a line sees an even number of errors. A shorter line of errors behaves the same way inside: only the checks at its two ends fire.

This silent failure is a logical error, and how often it happens is what every decoder is measured by. It also shows what distance (section 2) is worth. On a grid of distance 5 a line needs five storing qubits. So three real errors can be enough. A decoder that sees them may add two more.

6. Where the standard method goes wrong

The standard program misses the answer that can happen in two ways.

We went through every possible error on the smallest grid and compared two answers for each pattern of fired checks: the one the standard decoder gives, and the one most likely to be right. Of the 256 possible patterns on the smallest grid, 103 get an answer from the standard decoder that isn't the most likely one. That sounds like a lot, but these patterns are rare: together they turn up in fewer than 2 cases in 100.The smallest grid again.

The standard decoder is called matching, and the smallest grid shows how it works: 9 storing qubits, never read directly, and 8 checks between them, each asking its neighbours one question.

In the middle of the grid, a single flip fires the two flip-checks on either side of it. The phase-checks stay quiet: a flip isn't their kind of error. The decoder never sees the flip, only two lit checks.A flip in the middle lights the two squares beside it.

Now put several errors in a line. Each check along the line sees two of them, an even number, and stays quiet. Only the checks at the two ends fire. At the edge of the grid a line can end without firing anything.A second flip below it: the check between them goes dark; only the end of the line lights.

Decoding then becomes a pairing puzzle: join every fired check to another fired check, or to the edge. Matching picks the pairing with the shortest lines in total, since a few errors are more likely than many. Fast, exact methods exist for it, and almost every experiment uses it. Plain matching has two blind spots.Three checks lit; the standard program pairs them up, as briefly as it can.

Plain matching solves the puzzle separately for flip-checks and phase-checks, so an error that is both at once (section 3) counts as two. And it seeks the single likeliest explanation, though explanations that differ only in a way the code doesn't care about (section 5) add up.

The first of our 103 patterns shows it. Three checks fire. Plain matching explains them with two errors: a flip in one corner and a phase error in the centre. It fits the checks, and it is right one time in three. But when the truth is the other answer, matching's correction and the real errors together cross the grid: a silent logical error, about two times in three. Two cautions: we gave every storing qubit a 5% chance of an error, far more than a real chip has, so the effect is easy to count, and we assumed the checks never report wrongly.Its answer: a flip in the corner and a phase error in the centre, one way to these lights, right 10 times in 30.

The same checks also fit a combined error in that corner, a flip and a phase error at once, which the figure marks Y, plus one phase error on the right. Counting Y as one error, this answer also has two, and on its own it is exactly as likely as plain matching's. What tips the balance is that it can happen in two ways. The phase error can sit in the far corner. Or it can sit one place above it, and the checks fire just the same. Together, the two ways are right about six times in ten. The rest are rarer explanations.The other answer can happen in two ways, both giving the same three lights: right 19 times in 30.

Real chips add trouble of the same sort: errors that hit two neighbouring qubits at once, and qubits that slip into a third state they should never be in. A decoder that learns can take them in. Plain matching's picture has no place for either.

7. The result that started us off

On Google's chip, each step to a bigger grid halved the errors.

Google first showed, in 2023, a grid of distance 5 that beat distance 3, but only just. A year later, on a new chip, the errors were finally below the threshold – the error rate from section 2 under which bigger grids win – and the effect was clear. Each step from distance 3 to 5 to 7 cut the rate of logical errors roughly in half. At distance 7 the logical qubit lasted more than twice as long as the best single qubit on the chip.Google's grids at distances 3, 5 and 7; under each, its errors, roughly halving with every step up.

The factor by which the error shrinks per step is written Λ (lambda), and it decides how big a useful machine has to be. With Λ near 2, halving the error once more means going from distance 7 to distance 9: from 97 qubits to 161. That one halving costs 64 more qubits.A distance-9 grid would need 64 more qubits for one more halving.

Google read the same recorded data with two decoders. Its best matching decoder already links the two kinds of check in a limited way – the idea behind the correlated matching we use in section 9. It learns no rules of its own, though its error weights were tuned on the chip's data. It gave Λ = 2.04, and a neural network gave Λ = 2.14. At distance 7 the matching decoder's logical error rate was 171 per 100,000 rounds and the network's 143, about one in six fewer. Same chip, same data; only the reader changed.The network's errors beside the matching decoder's: at distance 7, 143 against 171 per 100,000 rounds.

Other labs have got below the threshold with other hardware, some with decoders that learn, and IBM has shown in simulation a fast one that doesn't. None of these results settles which route is best.

8. Accurate, or in time

[figure clock: not built yet]

Nobody writes a neural decoder's rules; it learns them. It's trained first on a simulator, where the true errors are known, then on the chip's own data, where only the outcome is known: whether the stored value came out as it went in. From this it picks up whatever regularities are there, including the ones matching leaves out.

The clock is where it struggles. The chip asks its checks once every 1.1 millionths of a second, about 900,000 times a second. A live decoder has to handle each round in that time.

Google's paper quotes 24 millionths of a second per round for its network, from earlier work. In that time the chip finishes about 22 more rounds. The network is accurate, but each answer comes late.

By the time the network has answered round 3, at 72 millionths of a second, the chip is at round 65. An interpreter who needs twenty-two seconds for every second of speech is more than twenty minutes behind after one minute. And the gap keeps growing.

So the network's results were worked out afterwards, on recorded data. The decoder that ran live was a fast, plain version of matching, and it kept pace. On a smaller chip, at distance 5 and on the same data, live matching's logical error rate was 350 per 100,000 rounds and the network's, afterwards, 269. Part of that gap is live against offline, not only matching against network.

A preprint from December 2025 reports a network that keeps pace: under one millionth of a second per round, on the kind of accelerator chips that can simply be bought, for grids up to distance 11. As far as we know other researchers haven't reviewed it yet, and we can't test it ourselves. Until then it stays a claim.

9. What we measured ourselves

We can't build a chip and we can't match Google's network. We can measure, in simulation, what a small neural decoder costs to train and run. The short answer: on the smallest grid our network soon beats plain matching; one grid larger it needs about 250 million examples to do so, and never catches correlated matching. We simulate the surface code with Stim, a free simulator written at Google, and use its standard noise model.

We wanted three measurements. First, whether a small network beats matching, and how many more examples it needs at distance 5 than at 3. Second, how long each decoder takes per run of three or five rounds on the same machine, next to how often it's wrong. Third, whether a network trained in simulation still works on the recordings Google has made public, before and after it has seen some of them. We did the first, part of the second, and not the third.

What we ran. One simulated run stores a logical qubit for as many rounds of checks as the grid's distance: three rounds at distance 3, five at distance 5. Every operation in the simulated circuit goes wrong with the same chance, which we call the noise level. Unless we say otherwise it is 0.5%, five times the one slip per thousand that the estimate in section 1 assumed. Every training step draws 4,096 fresh runs at one of seven noise levels between 0.2% and 1%, and afterwards every decoder reads the same 2 million fresh runs at each noise level. No training batch is reused. The network is small and plain: three layers of 256 units. That makes 138,241 adjustable numbers at distance 3 and 162,817 at distance 5, few enough to train in minutes on a GPU. It is told only which checks fired, never how the grid is laid out. It gives one answer per run: was the stored value flipped or not.

We compare against two versions of matching from PyMatching, the standard matching program. One is the plain version of section 6. The other is correlated matching, which solves the puzzle in two passes and lets the first inform the second. That is a limited way of linking the two kinds of check, like the one mentioned in section 7. Both are given an estimate of how often each kind of error happens: here, the exact one from the simulator, an advantage matching won't have on a chip. The comparison leans towards matching.

Each training was repeated from different random starting points, or seeds: eight at distance 3, five at distance 5.

Our small network passed the standard program one grid bigger only at the end of a run nearly four times longer, and still trailed the better free program.

Distance 3. Here the network beats plain matching, and early. After about 66 million examples it failed in 1.48% of runs, its eight seeds between 1.46% and 1.49%. Plain matching failed in 1.72% and correlated matching in 1.66%. The curves first dip below plain matching at 6.6 million examples in four seeds and at 9.3 million in the other four. By 13 million all eight were at or below correlated matching. That second win we don't count. In our library version correlated matching mishandles six single errors, and by a first-order estimate that costs it about the whole gap of 0.18 percentage points (see "A finding we didn't plan for").Distance 3: after 66 million examples our network is wrong less often than both matching programs.

Distance 5, the same 66 million examples. One grid larger, the same network loses in all five seeds. It failed in 1.90% to 2.16% of runs, plain matching in 1.41% and correlated matching in 1.09%. That is about a third to a half more failures than plain matching, and 1.7 to 2 times as many as correlated matching. The seeds also spread more than at distance 3.Distance 5, the same 66 million examples: it is wrong more often than both.

Distance 5, about 250 million examples. Trained longer, it passes plain matching, not correlated matching. The curves were still falling at 66 million, so we trained it again from scratch on nearly four times as many. The five long runs failed in 1.29% to 1.36% of runs, 1.33% together, or about one run in 75, against 1.41% for plain matching and 1.09% for correlated matching. That is 4% to 8% fewer failures than one, and a fifth to a quarter more than the other.Distance 5, about 250 million examples: it passes plain matching only at the end, still behind correlated matching.

At distance 3 the best answer for every pattern can simply be looked up. A table built from 200 million runs per noise level, a hundred times the test set, holds the more frequent answer for each pattern of fired checks. It failed in 1.47% of runs. Whether its runs overlap the test runs, and how it answers a pattern it never saw, our result files don't record. That table is our estimate of the best possible; since we can't say whether it saw the test runs, it may flatter itself slightly. The network's 1.48% is barely above it.

A finding we didn't plan for. We had expected correlated matching to be at least as good as plain matching everywhere. At distance 5 it was, at every noise level we tried. At distance 3 and low noise it was worse. On 4 million runs at a noise level of 0.1%, with both decoders reading the same runs, plain matching failed in 0.075% (0.073% to 0.078%). Correlated matching failed in 0.113% (0.109% to 0.116%), about one and a half times as many failures. At 0.3% it was still worse, 0.705% against 0.659%. From 0.4% upwards it was the better one. When we stored the other kind of value, it was again worse at 0.1%: 0.0986% against 0.0856%.

We first suspected our script. Two checks say it isn't at fault. The first: the simulator's error model for this circuit lists 219 error mechanisms. We applied each of them alone and decoded it; at distance 3 a decoder should survive every one. Plain matching corrects all 219. Correlated matching, in the version we used (PyMatching 2.4.0, called the way its documentation says), gets 6 of them wrong. Together those six happen with a chance of 0.4 times the noise level. At a noise level of 0.1% that is 0.04%, about the gap we measured, 0.037%. With the other kind of stored value it gets 4 of 221 wrong, 0.27 times the noise level. The second check: a second script, written by an AI agent from only a description of the test and without seeing ours, found the same six.

At distances 5 and 7 no single error defeats correlated matching. At distance 5 we also tried every pair of errors: it fails on 1,253 of 1,405,326 pairs, about one pair in 1,100, and plain matching on none. That pair check was not repeated independently. A search turned up no report of any of this, and we haven't traced the cause inside the library. So we state it for our setup only: this circuit, three rounds at distance 3, this version. At distance 3 and low noise it makes plain matching the harder comparison. That is why we always show both.

What we can answer, and what is still open.

Before starting we wrote down what would make us drop the idea: if the network needs so many more examples at distance 5 that a laptop can't train it, or if it loses on real recordings, then for a group like ours a neural decoder isn't worth its cost. We now have numbers for the first. On a laptop the long run would take under two hours per seed, so by the rule we set, the idea survives. But it took about 250 million examples to pass plain matching, and correlated matching, which needs no training and is free to download, stayed about a fifth ahead. The second test, real recordings, is still open.The opening drawing returns with our own small reader: learning to read is affordable on a laptop, but one grid bigger it passed plain matching only at the end of a run four times longer, still behind correlated matching. On real recordings it is not tested yet.

10. What this doesn't show

A decoder doesn't make a quantum algorithm faster in itself. It lets a calculation run longer before an error ruins it, and a better one means fewer qubits for the same reliability. A slow one holds up every step that waits for it.

Our numbers come from small grids and from a network far smaller than Google's. They say something about how the cost of learning grows. About larger grids they say nothing.
