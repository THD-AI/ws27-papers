# Questions to expect, and short answers

For the five minutes after the talk. The questions are the ones the round 11 readers (the course professor and an AI-sceptical listener) said they would ask. Each answer uses only facts already in the article and the deck; the backup slide that helps is named. Say them in your own words, not from this page.

## 1. Slide 4 says nobody can tell how much was the agents. Why copy the rebuild, then?

I can't prove the rebuild caused T-Mobile's result either. I argue it from need: an agent that changes a tariff has to know whose account it is. With fourteen ways to prove who you are, it can't. Telekom's own slide says the same thing: simplify before automating. (B1, slide 7)

## 2. Is an agent a transformation (slide 2) or incremental innovation (slide 16)?

Both. For the company, the agent is a transformation: the work moves from people answering to software acting. The way in is incremental: step by step, each key after the last one proved itself. In adoption terms, the three bricks are the organisation (the staff and the know-how), the environment (the rules and what people think of monitoring) and the technology (fourteen ways in, the suppliers).

## 3. What is the stop threshold in step 1?

Compare cost per finished request for the same kinds of cases. Count a request as finished if it was resolved and the customer doesn't come back about the same issue within seven days. If the agent's cost isn't lower, or errors or reversals rise, Telekom stops at the current stage. The exact figure is Telekom's to set; I don't have its internal costs.

## 4. "No dismissals" – but 4,000 positions a year still go. Isn't that replacement, only slower?

Partly, yes, and that's why the title says it. Positions go by agreed routes, and any saving has to fit into those 4,000 a year. My point is what the agents are for: the people who stay are the first to train, and the agent works with them. At T-Mobile, in about three of four conversations, the staff see the system's recommendations. (Slide 6 notes, B1 row 6)

## 5. Does the agreement cover outsourced call-centre staff? What happens after 2028?

It covers the employees under Telekom's collective agreements; the union puts it at about 60,000. I didn't find what applies to contractors, and I found nothing agreed beyond 2028. That gap is the second thing that would prove me wrong: if the teams shrink faster than the agreed routes allow.

## 6. What does it cost me, the customer, when the agent gets it wrong?

That's why the keys come in stages. First the agent only reads. Then it does what can be undone. Last come the changes a fraudster could use, like a new bank account, and the customer confirms those in the app. The system checks every key, not the model. And there is always a way to a person. (Slide 15, B3)

## 7. T-Mobile is your good example. What happened to its staff, and why is its satisfaction lower?

T-Mobile reported $531 million of severance up to September 2026, in a restructuring that reduced organisational layers and duplicate roles; its filings don't tie that to the agents. The satisfaction scores aren't comparable: T-Mobile's 46 and Telekom's "more than 67" are two different kinds of score. (B1 rows 8 and 10)

## 8. Why are people part of the wall?

The wall is everything already there that the agent has to work with: the systems, the rules, the people. In Germany you don't build around the people; they decide how the cable goes in. That's the works council on slide 11.

## 9. Frag Magenta already acts on accounts. Doesn't that undercut "it can't act on an account it can't identify"?

No, it's the first case of step 6. After login, Frag Magenta can already show a bill or change the bank account on a mobile contract; otherwise it hands over to staff. Login is one of the fourteen ways in. The fourteen block it across channels and countries: on the phone, in a shop, in another country. One customer record is what lets the same agent act everywhere.
