# Reader round 11 – the AI-sceptical listener

Seat: a fellow student who has worked part-time in a German call centre, knows people whose jobs were automated, distrusts press releases and has sat through too many "AI will transform everything" talks. Not hostile. Read: all 46 pages in `.shots/deck/renovate-v2/` and `CUES-v2.md`.

## Scores

| | Score | Why, in one line |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Visuals | 8 | Calm, honest charts; the dashed "not published" gaps (p07, p09) are the most trustworthy thing in the deck. |
| Argument | 6 | The analysis is careful, but the talk asks a board question ("can Telekom copy the 32%?") and never defends the worker thesis it rests on. |
| Reading | 7 | Plain, unhyped language; two headlines (p21, p16) say more than their own numbers support. |
| Overall | 7 | Good, with clear flaws. Not cheerleading. But workers and customers appear as constraints on a rollout, not as people the talk is for. |

## First impression

This is not a sales pitch. The speaker corrects his own number in minute two (p05–p07: 40 becomes 32, and "how many the software finished: not published"), labels company figures as company figures, admits nobody outside can tell what the agents did (p09), shows two walk-backs (p20), and ends with three ways he could be wrong (p39). He even discloses that the drawings were generated (p02). That earns a lot of trust from me early.

What loses it again is framing. Every source of success is an investor-day deck. The opening question and the closing line are both about a company KPI. The speaker's own thesis – renovate so workers are enabled, not replaced – is never said out loud in the main talk. A sceptic hears a well-sourced talk about how Telekom can get to 32% more carefully than Klarna did. "More carefully" is not the same as "for the workers".

## Slide by slide, where I'd react

### p01–p02, title and opening
- Problem: the question is "Can Telekom copy what T-Mobile's agents did?" That is the investor's question. Nobody in the room works for the investor.
- Fix: add one spoken sentence after the question – "and can it do that without doing to its call-centre staff what Klarna tried?" – so the worker stake is in the question from the start.

### p03–p04, slide 2: chatbot versus agent
- Good: concrete, small example ("move me to a cheaper tariff").
- Problem: the example is exactly where agents hurt customers – the wrong tariff, a new 24-month minimum term, a lost discount, a cancelled add-on. The slide shows the happy path only.
- Fix: add one line under "a key to change it": "and if it picks the wrong tariff, the customer pays for it."

### p05–p07, slide 3: 40 versus 32
- The strongest trust moment in the talk. Keep it.
- Small point: "Still, one in three is a lot" (notes) quietly turns a number you just said you can't verify into an achievement. Say "one in three meet the software first" and leave the judgement out.

### p08–p09, slide 4: 7.0 to 3.1
- Problem: "fewer contacts" is presented as a good thing. From the customer's side, fewer contacts can also mean customers gave up, or that it got harder to reach anyone. You never say which.
- Fix: one spoken clause – "fewer contacts can mean fewer problems, or fewer people who bothered; the number can't tell."

### p10–p13, slide 5: cable, wall, renovate, repaint
- Helps: the picture is memorable and makes "same technology, different groundwork" stick. The footnote "The walls picture conditions; they are not evidence" is honest.
- Hides, and this is the one I'd raise my hand about: the italic line says "The wall is what's already there: old systems, rules, people." So people are the brick you cut into. For a talk whose thesis is "enable workers, not replace them", putting the workers inside the wall you open with a chisel is the wrong picture. It also makes the US timber frame – where nothing stands in the way, including staff protection – look like the clean, easy case.
- Fix: take "people" out of the wall. "The wall is old systems and rules. The people are the ones who live in the house – and the ones who know where the old pipes run."

### p14–p16, slide 6: T-Mobile rebuilt
- Good: "Not reaching a person isn't the same as resolved" is on the slide.
- Problem 1: the notes then say "A real result." For a customer, 78% of chats that never reached a person is just as likely to be a customer stuck in a loop. The slide is fair; the spoken line undoes it.
- Problem 2, the big one: what happened to T-Mobile's own service staff while it rebuilt? Your backup B1 (p42) has it – row 10, "cost of staff change: $531 m", and row 8, satisfaction 46 at T-Mobile against more than 67 at Telekom. Neither number is in the main talk. If the model case for "renovate" also shed workers and has the lower satisfaction score, the room deserves to hear that, from you, before someone finds it in the Q&A.
- Fix: one line on p16 or in the notes: "Renovating didn't stop T-Mobile paying $531 million for staff changes – I can't tie that to the agents, and neither can anyone outside." Drop "a real result".

### p17–p18, slide 7: 14 ways to 3
- Fine, and "Telekom's own words" is fair attribution.

### p19–p20, slide 8: Klarna and Commonwealth Bank
- Good and fair, and this is the only place the talk shows what happens to workers.
- Missing: why Commonwealth Bank walked back. As far as I know it was the union challenging it that forced the reversal. That is your thesis winning in real life – staff having a say – and you don't say it.
- Fix: one clause: "withdrawn after the staff's union challenged it."
- Also: "the saving usually comes from the staff" is the sharpest worker sentence in the talk. It is only in the notes. Put it on the slide.

### p21, slide 9: "First brick: the staff stay." – fig leaf or convincing?
Mostly a fig leaf as currently worded, and the slide itself shows why.
- The headline says the staff stay. The chart directly under it shows about 4,000 full-time positions leaving every year, 20,223 in five years. "No dismissals" means nobody is fired; it does not mean the jobs stay. The notes say "Any saving has to fit into that" – which means: the agents save money by not replacing people who leave. That is replacement, done slowly and politely. Honest, maybe even the best realistic outcome, but it is not "the staff stay".
- Scope: the agreement covers about 60,000 under Telekom's collective agreements. A lot of German telecom customer contact runs through subsidiaries and outside service providers. The people I worked with in a call centre were not employees of the brand on the phone. Are they inside the 60,000? If not, they are exactly who an agent replaces first, with no protection until 2028.
- "Until 31 December 2028" – and Telekom's target is 2030. What happens in 2029 and 2030?
- "The people who stay are the first ones to train" – an opinion, stated without support.
- Fix: retitle to "First brick: no one is fired, but jobs still go" (or "the staff stay; the positions don't"), and say in one sentence whether contractors are covered and what happens after 2028. That turns a fig leaf into a defended point, and it is more convincing, not less.

### p22, slide 10: 79% on monitoring
- Good worker-side evidence.
- Problem: "On AI and jobs, Germans are no more pessimistic than Americans" – backup B4 (p45) shows 61% of Germans expect AI to eliminate jobs. "No more pessimistic" hides that a clear majority are pessimistic. It reads as "Germans aren't scared, just picky about monitoring".
- Fix: "Six in ten Germans expect AI to cost jobs – about as many as Americans. Where they differ is monitoring."

### p23–p25, slide 11: checks come first
- Good: the works-council gate and the 2021 framework (no individual monitoring, groups larger than five) are real worker protections, clearly shown.
- Problem: the only customer-side protection named is being told you are talking to an AI system (AI Act Art. 50). Being told is not being helped. Is there a right to reach a human? A complaint route when the agent gets it wrong? Who pays when it does?
- Fix: add one line to the notes: "Being told is the minimum. The plan below adds a way back to a person (step 6)." – and then actually add it to step 6.
- "No staff say found before launch" for T-Mobile is honestly worded ("found"). Keep.

### p26–p27, slide 12: missing knowledge
- Fine. "Without your own people, you can't cut it in" is the closest the talk comes to saying workers are an asset, not an obstacle. Good line; it contradicts the "people are the wall" line on p13, which is another reason to fix p13.

### p28–p30, slide 13: eight steps
- Problem: step 1 measures cost per contact and repeat contacts – the business measure – and says so proudly ("not accuracy"). There is no customer measure: complaints, wrong changes undone, time to reach a person. There is no worker measure either beyond "per team, never per person" (which is good).
- Step 4 "Train staff" – train them for what job? The thesis is "workers enabled by AI". Not one slide shows what a service worker's day looks like with the agent beside them. Your backup has the evidence: B1 row 6, "help for staff: 74% / more than 12,000". That is your thesis in a number and it is hidden.
- Fix: add "complaints and time to a person" next to cost per contact in step 1, and move the staff-assist number (B1 row 6) into slide 13 or 6.

### p31–p33, slide 14: "the cost of getting it wrong"
- The cue sheet calls this build "the cost of getting it wrong". The cost shown is $278 million written off by T-Mobile. That is the company's cost of a failed IT project. It is not what an agent getting it wrong costs a customer.
- Your brief question to me: does the talk say what an agent getting it wrong costs a real customer? No. Nowhere in the 15 minutes. The nearest is p36 ("fraud-prone: confirmed by the customer in the app") and the backup B3 on prompt injection. A pensioner switched to the wrong tariff, a customer whose bank details are changed by a fraudster who talked the agent round, a disabled customer who can't use the app confirmation – none appear.
- Fix: one concrete customer case on p33 or p36, one sentence: "If the agent changes the wrong account, a customer pays a bill that isn't theirs until someone notices; that is why changes come last."

### p34–p36, slide 15: keys in stages
- Good: "The model can ask; the system decides" is the best safety line in the talk and it is concrete. Read, undoable, fraud-prone is a sensible order.
- Missing: a "hand to a person" key – the customer can always leave the agent. And the app confirmation assumes every customer has and can use the app.
- Fix: add a fourth line: "always: a way to a person, without starting over."

### p37–p40, slide 16: three things would prove me wrong
- Very good for trust. Item 2 ("service teams shrink faster than the agreed routes allow") is the right worker test.
- Problem: there is no customer test. If complaints rise or satisfaction falls after the agents, would that prove you wrong? It should.
- Closing line: "Telekom can't import the 32%. It can copy the slow work behind it." Ends on the company KPI again. The thesis is never stated.
- Fix: make the last spoken sentence the thesis: "Renovate so the people in the house can use the new cable – not so the house needs fewer of them."

## Does it come across as fair, or as cheerleading?

Fair on numbers, not cheerleading in tone. One-sided in perspective: the talk is told from the investor deck outward. Workers appear as a brick (constraint) and a gate (works council); customers appear as a source of contacts to reduce and as an identity to verify. The speaker's own belief – workers enabled, not replaced – is the most interesting part of what he thinks, and it is the part he defends least.

## Does the analogy help or hide?

It helps with "same technology, different groundwork", and the Germany-as-brick picture makes the slow path feel like craft, not failure. It hides two things: it puts people in the wall (p13), and it makes "renovating" sound neutral, when in practice the renovation is paid for partly by 4,000 positions a year not refilled (p21). An analogy is fine; just don't let it carry the worker question for you.

## What would make me trust the speaker less

1. Saying "the staff stay" over a chart of 20,000 positions leaving (p21).
2. Saying "a real result" about 78% of chats never reaching a person (p16 notes).
3. Having T-Mobile's $531 m staff-change cost and its lower satisfaction score in the backup but not in the talk (p42). If I found that in Q&A, I'd assume the rest was curated too.

## Where I would raise my hand, and what I'd ask

1. (p21) "The headline says the staff stay, the chart says 4,000 a year go. Are the agents meant to save money by not replacing people who leave? Then isn't that replacement, just slower?"
2. (p21) "Does the no-dismissals agreement cover the outsourced call-centre workers who actually answer Telekom's phones, or only the 60,000 under its collective agreements? And what happens in 2029, before the 2030 target?"
3. (p33/p36) "What does it cost me, the customer, when the agent gets it wrong – a wrong tariff, a changed bank account? Who do I complain to, and can I always get a human?"
4. (p16/p42) "T-Mobile is your good example. What happened to its service staff, and why is its satisfaction score lower than Telekom's?"
5. (p13) "Your step 1 measures cost per contact. Where is the measure for the customer and for the worker? What would stop Telekom at step 1 for customer reasons?"
6. (thesis) "You say this is about enabling workers. Can you show one thing a service worker does better with the agent – not just fewer contacts?"
7. (p13) "Why are people part of the wall you cut into?"

## The three things that cost the most

1. p21, "the staff stay" headline contradicts its own chart (4,000 positions a year) and ignores contractors and post-2028 – fix: retitle "No one is fired; positions still go" and say in one sentence who is covered and until when.
2. No customer cost anywhere – "the cost of getting it wrong" (p33) is a company write-off, not a customer harm – fix: one concrete customer case on p33 or p36 and a "way to a person, always" line in step 6.
3. The worker thesis is never stated or evidenced, while the evidence against it (T-Mobile $531 m staff change, satisfaction 46) and for it (staff-assist 74% / >12,000) sit unused in backup B1 – fix: move both into slides 6 and 13 and end the talk on the thesis, not the 32%.
