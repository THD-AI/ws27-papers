## Overall judgement

A disciplined, honest case study with unusually good sourcing for a bachelor's talk. Every data slide carries its source and says "company figure, not audited". He corrects his own proposal on stage (slide 3, 40 vs 32), and he ends with falsifiable conditions (slide 16). The house picture carries the talk and is used consistently from slide 1 to the end.

What holds it back from a 1.0–1.3: (a) the answer to the opening question isn't stated until minute 14, (b) the course theory is thin on screen, although the article has it (TOE, absorptive capacity, J-curve) and the talk drops all three, and (c) there is a gap between "nobody can tell how much was the agents" (slide 4) and "copy the slow work behind it" (slide 16) that he never closes out loud.

## 1. Is the thesis clear in the first minute, and at the end?

- **First minute: the question, not the thesis.** Page 2 ends on "Can Telekom copy what T-Mobile's agents did?" The title "Renovate, don't repaint" is a slogan whose meaning only arrives at 3:20 (slide 5). At 1:00 I know the topic and the question but not his answer. The article states it in its second paragraph ("it can't be bought and bolted onto a company"); the talk doesn't.
  - Fix: one sentence after the question on page 2: "My answer: not the number, but the work behind it, and I'll show which work."
- **At the end: clear.** Page 40 ("Telekom can't import the 32%. It can copy the slow work behind it.") is a strong, quotable closing line and answers the question.

## 2. Does the argument hold from evidence to recommendation?

The chain is: T-Mobile's number is narrower than claimed (3) → the outcome can't be attributed (4) → T-Mobile rebuilt systems alongside the agents (6) → Telekom hasn't yet (7) → repainting fails (8) → the German wall has three bricks (9–12) → an eight-step order (13–15) → falsifiers (16). It is well built. Weak joints:

- **Slide 4 → slide 16, attribution.** He shows honestly that the fall from 7.0 to 3.1 can't be attributed to the agents. He then recommends copying the rebuilding, which is equally unattributed. The article says it plainly ("T-Mobile's sequence can't be shown to have caused its result; it follows from what each step needs from the one before"). In the talk the only hedge is "The order is my hypothesis" (slides 13, 15). A grader will notice that the recommendation is justified by logic (an agent can't act on an account it can't identify), not by T-Mobile's evidence. That is a fine basis, but he has to say so.
  - Fix: on slide 14 or 16, one sentence: "I can't prove the rebuilding caused T-Mobile's result. I argue it from need: an agent can't act on an account it can't identify."
- **Slide 8, two cherry-picked cases.** Klarna and Commonwealth Bank are labelled "Repainting, twice", but the talk doesn't show that nothing changed behind their agents. It infers that from the staff cuts. The article admits "two cases don't make a rule" and that neither is US or German; the talk drops both caveats. Selecting cases on the outcome is a classic weak point a grader will mark.
  - Fix: add "Two cases, neither American nor German, don't make a rule; they show the route" to the notes for slide 8.
- **Slide 12, transfer of the Destatis survey.** The 72% "missing knowledge" comes from firms with ten or more employees, mostly SMEs, that considered AI and didn't adopt it. Telekom is a large group with its own AI units. The brick applies, but not as stated.
  - Fix: one clause in the notes: "For Telekom the gap isn't in the lab; it's in the service teams and the works council who have to run and check it."
- **Slide 9, "helps more than it hurts".** This is an opinion and is marked "I think". Good. Keep it.
- **Slide 11, US row.** "No staff say found before launch" is an absence-of-evidence claim, hedged correctly in speech ("I found"). The on-screen wording is clumsy.
  - Fix: "no staff say before launch that I could find".

## 3. Course concepts: correct and visible enough?

Visible on screen: change vs transformation (slide 2), the business measure over accuracy (slide 13), a process model (slide 15), incremental vs radical innovation via Hall and Soskice (slide 16), regulation (slide 11, B2). That is enough to pass the "uses the course" bar, but too thin and too late for a top mark.

- **Adoption theory is missing from the talk, although the article has it.** The three bricks are exactly TOE (Tornatzky and Fleischer 1990): staff and know-how (organisation), law and attitudes (environment), the 14 identity paths and the suppliers (technology). Brick 3 is absorptive capacity. Step 5 before the return is the productivity J-curve. All three are in the article and none is in the talk. This is the cheapest marks in the deck.
  - Fix: on slide 9 (first brick), one subtitle line: "Adoption research sorts barriers into technology, organisation and environment; here are Telekom's." Name absorptive capacity once on slide 12.
- **Transformation (slide 2) vs incremental (slide 16) is a contradiction a grader will test.** Slide 2 says an agent is "a transformation, not a change". Slide 16 says Germany is better at incremental innovation and "renovating is incremental work". Both can be true (a transformational technology introduced by an incremental path), but he never says so.
  - Fix: on page 40, half a sentence: "The agent transforms the work; the way in is incremental."
- **Slide 2's use of "transformation" is thin.** It is justified only by "it acts". In the course, transformation means a change in how the organisation works, not a property of the software. His own slide 6 (contractors → own engineers, one customer record) is the actual transformation evidence.
  - Fix: in the slide 2 notes: "...a transformation, not a change: it changes who does the work, not just how fast."
- **Process model, slide 15: name it.** "A project life cycle with a works-council gate and a stop point" is vague. What he describes is a stage-gate model (Cooper) with a go/kill gate at step 1 and a co-determination gate at steps 2 and 7.
  - Fix: write "stage-gate" on the slide and point at the two gates on the house.
- **Types of innovation are never named.** This is a process innovation (service delivery), with a business-model angle (cost per contact). One word on slide 2 would place it in the course's typology.

## 4. Sources and hedging

Strong. Every data slide has a footer source, company figures are marked as such, targets are marked as targets (slides 7, 14, B1), the 40 vs 32 correction is exemplary, and the drawings are disclosed as generated (page 1). The source list (B5) is complete for what is on the slides.

Gaps:
- B5 has only one academic source (Hall and Soskice). For an innovation-management course I'd expect the adoption frame (TOE or Rogers) and absorptive capacity to be listed. They are in the article; add them to B5 once they are on slides 9 and 12.
- Slides 15 and B3 have no source footer. The staged-permission design is his own proposal; say "own proposal" in the footer, so it isn't read as an unsourced claim.
- Slide 11: AI Act Art. 50 is cited "as amended by 2026/1744". Expect a question on what the amendment changed; B2 gives dates but not that.
- Slide 4: the "mid-2026" bar is drawn just after the 2026 tick, not at mid-year. Minor, but a data person will notice.

## 5. Time: does it fit 15 minutes?

- The notes total about 1,420 words, about 11 minutes at 130 wpm. The cue sheet ends the last content slide at about 14:30 (start 13:40 plus about 50 seconds). The gap of about 3.5 minutes is in pauses and build clicks. That is realistic for a calm speaker, but under exam nerves students speed up, and he could finish at 12:00–12:30. Finishing early costs less than overrunning, but a 12-minute talk in a 15-minute slot reads as underprepared.
- The slack is in the right place for the fixes above. The thesis sentence, the TOE line, the attribution sentence and the transformation/incremental half-sentence add about 60 words, about 30 seconds. Don't add more than that.
- Per slide, from note length against the cue gap:
  - Slide 10 (79%) has 25 s planned for about 31 s of speech: it will overrun.
    - Fix: cut "On AI and jobs, Germans are no more pessimistic than Americans" from speech (it is on screen and in B4).
  - Slides 7 and 14 are tight (about 20 s and 40 s for about 21 s and 38 s of speech). Fine, but no room to stumble.
  - Slide 1 has 55 s for about 35 s of speech, and slide 5 has 80 s for about 58 s. Those are the natural places for the thesis sentence and for breathing.
- Rehearse once with a stopwatch at the 7:45 mark (slide 10). If he is ahead of the cue there, he should slow down on the steps, not add content.

## 6. Questions I'd ask (and whether the backups answer them)

1. **"Slide 4 says nobody can tell how much was the agents. Why should Telekom copy T-Mobile's rebuilding, if that can't be shown to have caused the result either?"** No backup answers it directly. B1 shows the numbers don't compare; it doesn't defend the recommendation. He needs a spoken answer: the argument comes from need (an agent can't act on an account it can't identify), plus Telekom's own slide ("simplify before automating"), not from T-Mobile's result.
2. **"You call agents a transformation on slide 2 and renovation incremental on slide 16. Which is it, and which adoption model are your three bricks?"** No backup. The answer is in the article (TOE; a transformational technology by an incremental route). A one-row backup mapping the bricks to TOE would close it.
3. **"Step 1 says stop if there is no gain. What is the threshold, and what does the customer record cost compared with the saving?"** Partly covered by B1 row 9 (about €2.5 bn outlook) and slide 14 ($278 m write-off), but there is no stop criterion. He should have a number or a rule ready, for example "cost per finished request below today's cost per contact, with repeat contacts not rising".

Also likely, and covered: "Are Germans just against AI?" (B4, good); "Which law applies when?" (B2, good); "Prompt injection and fraud?" (B3, adequate but unsourced); "Why not compare the numbers directly?" (B1, good).

Not covered and plausible: "Isn't the Destatis survey about small firms?" (see 2); "What did the AI Act amendment 2026/1744 change?" (see 4).

## 7. What would cost him marks

- No stated answer in the first minute (thesis only implicit in the title).
- Course theory mostly implicit: no adoption model on screen, process model not named, transformation vs incremental unreconciled.
- The recommendation rests on reasoning, while the talk frames it as copying T-Mobile, whose causality he himself shows is unknown.
- Two hand-picked repainting cases without the "two cases don't make a rule" caveat.
- Timing risk from nerves: about 11 minutes of words in a 15-minute slot.

## The three costliest, each with a one-line fix

1. **Course theory hidden (adoption, transformation vs incremental, process model unnamed).** Fix: put "technology, organisation, environment (TOE)" on slide 9, "stage-gate" on slide 15, and "the agent transforms; the way in is incremental" on page 40.
2. **Recommendation not tied to evidence (slide 4 shows no attribution; slide 16 says copy the work).** Fix: one sentence on slide 14 or 16: "I can't prove the rebuild caused T-Mobile's result; I argue it from need: an agent can't act on an account it can't identify."
3. **Thesis not stated in the first minute.** Fix: after the question on page 2, say: "My answer: not the number, but the slow work behind it."

## The three questions I'd ask

1. If nobody can attribute T-Mobile's result to the agents or to the rebuild, why copy the rebuild?
2. Is an AI agent a transformation (slide 2) or incremental innovation (slide 16), and which adoption model are your three bricks?
3. What is the stop threshold in step 1, and how does the cost of one customer record compare with the expected saving?
