# Reader r11 – voice, pacing and read-aloud

Seat: voice-and-pacing and read-aloud reader. Material read: `BRAND/methods/his-pacing.md` in full; seven dictations (15-001, 15-009, 15-013, 16-017, 16-020, 18-003, 19-002); `CUES-v2.md` (cue sheet and every note); slides p01–p41 (all final builds, plus the middle builds of 5, 11 and 13).

## Verdict in one paragraph

Most of the time it sounds like him. The father's wall, the correction to his own proposal, "sixteen every working day", "you can screw it onto the wall, you can't cut it in" and the Hall and Soskice turn are all moves from his dictations: set the ground, hold a physical picture, land short. Three things pull it down. The steps (slides 13–15) are almost three minutes of process with three sentences of 31–39 words, and Alex disappears behind them. Five or six sentences use written-English grammar that nobody says out loud: semicolons, participle clauses, an elided "they are", "on that base". And the clock is off. The notes speak in about 12 to 12.5 minutes, but the cue sheet plans 14:40. Some windows are too tight (slides 7, 10, 14) and others are too loose (3, 5, 11), so he will run early in some places and rush in others.

Speech alone takes 10.8 minutes. About 39 clicks at roughly 1.5 s each to look at the build adds a minute, and a short pause on each of the 16 landing lines adds about 25 s. **Estimated spoken length: 12 to 12.5 minutes.** A nervous first run speeds up (at 140 wpm it takes about 11.5); a careful non-native pace slows down (at 120 wpm, about 13). The cue sheet's 14:40 implies an effective pace near 105 wpm, which nobody keeps up on stage.

**Grow, or leave the gaps?** Grow a little, about 60 to 75 s, in three planned places (below), and land near 13:30. That leaves about 90 s of real buffer for nerves, a slow click or a laugh. Don't fill the rest with facts: the gaps on slides 3, 5 and 11 are healthy, because those are the slides with the most builds and the audience needs time to read them. Then **re-time the cue sheet from the measured words**. As it stands he will reach slide 7 early, slide 10 late and slide 14 out of breath, and a cue sheet that is wrong in both directions is worse than none.

Where the 60 to 75 s should go:
1. **Slide 5, one physical detail (about 10 s).** The analogy is held well, but it is all diagram and no dust. One working detail of the kind he gives in his dictations helps here. Sketch: *"Cutting in is loud and dusty. Afterwards you can't see the cable at all. The duct you see forever."* (Only if it matches his father's picture.)
2. **Slide 9, a held pause after "Sixteen every working day."** (2–3 s). It is the best number-to-person comparison in the talk, so let it sit.
3. **Slide 15, one beat of his own (about 15 s).** He builds software, so the key ladder is something he does, not something he read. Sketch, only if true: *"It's how I'd give any new script access. Read-only first. Then we see."*
4. **Slide 16, close the loop on the opening bias (about 10 s).** He opens with "I may see Germany as the harder case". The close never returns to it. Sketch, only if he agrees: *"I started out thinking Germany was the harder case. It is harder. That doesn't make it worse."*

## Slide by slide

**1 (title).** The first sentence opens on an institution, which his-pacing §4 says he never does: he opens on a situation or an opinion. *"On 5 October it told its investors about T-Mobile: software agents there now do a big part of customer service, and Telekom wants the same in Europe by 2030"* runs 29 words with a colon in the middle and is hard to say in one breath. "My position:" is a written label. Sketch: *"In October, Telekom stood in front of its investors and pointed at America. … Where I stand: I was born in the US, I study in Germany. So I may see Germany as the harder case."* The bias is named once and early, in the first person, which is right. Ending on the question works here because it is the hinge into the talk.

**2.** Good. The tariff scene is a small scene with dialogue (Unit 5), and it lands on a strong line. "In this course's terms, a transformation, not a change" is a verbless reference line. Say it as a sentence: *"In this course's words, that's a transformation, not just a change."*

**3.** The correction to his own proposal is very him (the "well, not X: Y" move), and "Still, one in three is a lot" is a good concede-then-turn. Two problems. The quoted phrase "served by an AI agent" can't be heard as a quote, so let the slide carry it or say "what they call served". The landing repeats the first sentence almost word for word, so it lands flat after "one in three is a lot". Either make "Still, one in three is a lot" the final spoken line, or say the title with a pause before it, as a verdict rather than a summary.

**4.** *"A customer account got in touch about seven times a year"* – an account doesn't get in touch. Sketch: *"Each customer contacted them about seven times a year."* "Less than half." is a good short beat. The landing is fine.

**5.** It is held for eight sentences and called back later (slides 8, 12, 14), which is exactly Unit 4. The trouble is the mapping run: *"The cable is the agent. The wall is what's already there… The duct is repainting. Cutting into the wall is renovating."* Four equations in a row, then the landing, makes six sentences of the same length and the beat turns metronomic. Fold two of them: *"The cable is the agent. The wall is everything already there: old systems, rules, people. The duct is repainting; cutting in is renovating."* Check that the four clicks line up with the spoken sentences (build 3 should fire on "Or you cut a channel").

**6.** *"Before, outside contractors worked on other companies' systems"* – by ear, "other companies" is unclear (whose?). The slide says suppliers' systems, so say that. "On that base" is written English; say "With that in place". "A real result." is good, but the slide adds that not reaching a person isn't the same as resolved, and the notes skip it. Sketch: *"Not the same as solved. Still a real result."* Name "one customer record" here, so that slide 14's landing is a callback and not a new term (his callback rule).

**7.** Twenty seconds for 19 s of speech plus a click is too tight. The fourteen-sockets image is good and fits the cable picture. Give this slide 10 s more on the cue sheet, taken from slide 3 or 11.

**8.** *"Said its assistant did the work of seven hundred service agents"* – "agents" has meant software for seven minutes, and here it suddenly means people. Say "seven hundred people in customer service". *"Forty-five roles declared redundant for a voice bot, the decision withdrawn within weeks"* is newspaper grammar built from participle clauses. Sketch: *"It cut forty-five jobs for a voice bot, and took it back within weeks."* "Had stopped hiring" is pluperfect for no reason; "stopped hiring" is enough. There is no grade of his own anywhere on this slide; the landing does that job, which is fine.

**9.** The strongest slide for voice: a hinge, three bricks, a big number with a felt comparison, then "I think…" and the landing. Two small fixes. "ver.di" means nothing to a non-German ear, so say "the union ver.di". *"People still go by agreed routes"* sounds odd; try *"people still leave, but only by agreed routes"*. Hold the pause after "Sixteen every working day."

**10.** This is the slide that overruns, and it says its own landing twice: "In Germany nearly four in five see it as a bad thing" comes three sentences before the identical title line. *"On AI and jobs, Germans are no more pessimistic than Americans; on monitoring at work they are"* has a semicolon and an elided comparative ("they are" what?). Nobody can catch that by ear. Sketch: *"On jobs, Germans worry about as much as Americans. On being watched at work, they worry more."* Cutting the first four-in-five sentence fixes both the duplicate and the clock. (The footnote says the survey was asked in the EU only, so check where the American comparison comes from before saying it aloud.)

**11.** *"German law makes it answer before launch"* – "it" points back across a slide change to "any new system", and after a click nobody remembers that. Say "makes every new system answer". *"At T-Mobile I found no say for the staff before launch"* – sketch: *"At T-Mobile, as far as I could find, the staff had no say before launch."* *"In 2021 it agreed the rules with its works council once: nobody gets watched as a person, and results are only looked at in groups of more than five, without names"* runs 31 words, and "once" reads as "formerly". Split it: *"In 2021 it agreed the rules with its works council, one time, for all its IT. Nobody is watched as a person. Results only in groups of more than five, no names."* "And here Telekom is ahead" is a good turn.

**12.** Destatis asking "why not?" is a small scene, which is good. Spell the source out for an English room: "the Federal Statistical Office". The ladder runs out of order. *"Without your own people who understand your systems, you can buy a language model"* puts the condition on the wrong rung. Reorder it so the break carries the meaning (Unit 6): *"You can buy a language model. You can screw it onto the wall. Without your own people, you can't cut it in."*

**13.** "So how would I do it?" is a perfect hinge question. After it come the two hardest sentences in the talk. Step one is 33 words with a semicolon and a "then, with the agent," aside. Steps three and four are 39 words that pack two steps, a statistic and a qualifier ("measuring teams, never single people") into one sentence. No non-native speaker says that in one breath, and the room loses step three. Sketch: *"Step three: keep the option to switch suppliers. About half of German firms depend strongly on American technology. Step four: train the staff before each new key. Measure teams, never single people."* This is also where he starts to disappear: for slides 13–15 the only "I" is the opening question and "my hypothesis".

**14.** *"It takes years, and it can fail: in the third quarter of 2025 T-Mobile wrote off two hundred seventy-eight million dollars on parts of a billing-system replacement it abandoned, about a tenth of its net income that quarter."* At 38 words, with "two hundred seventy-eight million" and a reduced relative clause, it is the worst mouthful in the deck. Sketch: *"It takes years, and it can fail. Last year T-Mobile gave up part of a new billing system. It wrote off about two hundred eighty million dollars. A tenth of that quarter's profit."* "This is the channel cut into the brick" is a good callback. The window is tight (37 s of speech, 40 s planned, 3 builds), and the split costs nothing in time.

**15.** "But reading doesn't have to wait" is a good turn, and "the model can ask, the system decides" is the best plain line in the second half. Steps seven and eight make a 31-word sentence of three listed clauses. *"As a process model, it's a project life cycle with a works-council gate and a stop point added"* is a sentence written for the grader. It reads as teaching rather than finding, and it is the densest sentence on the densest slide, at minute 13, when the room's attention is lowest. Cut it to *"In the course's terms: a life cycle, plus a works-council gate and a stop."* or move it to the slide text alone. Spend the time saved on his own beat (Grow, point 3).

**16.** Three "if" conditions in a row, with the frame ("Three things would prove me wrong") only at the end. Listeners have to hold each "if" without knowing why. This is the one slide where the "say the title at the last build" convention hurts: say the frame first, then the three ifs. The close is good. People say Germany is slow, then the turn, then "Renovating is step-by-step work", then the landing: concede, turn, land, his Unit 7 exactly. "Hall and Soskice" arrives cold, so say "two economists, Hall and Soskice". Pronunciation: SOS-kiss. Then the callback to the opening bias, and "Thank you."

## Is it him?

Mostly yes. Present: setting the ground before the claim (slides 2, 9, 12), the long physical analogy with callbacks, numbers turned into people (sixteen a day, one in three, four in five), the correction to his own work, "I think that helps more than it hurts", concede then turn. Missing or thin: his own reaction after runs of sourced claims on slides 4, 6, 10, 11 and 14 (his-pacing rule 3), and any first-person beat in the steps. The rhythm of the notes is evenly short. He goes three to six longer sentences, then one short landing, and the notes rarely let a sentence run long except where it shouldn't (13, 14, 15). The friend's "news outlet" remark is mostly fixed in the first half. It comes back in slides 10, 11 and 13–15.
