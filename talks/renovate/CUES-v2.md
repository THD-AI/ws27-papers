## Notes by slide

** (page 1).** In October, Telekom stood in front of its investors and pointed at America. At T-Mobile, software agents now do a big part of customer service, and Telekom wants the same in Europe by 2030. Where I stand: I was born in the US, and I study in Germany. So I may see Germany as the harder case. That's why every claim has a source. **Can Telekom copy what T-Mobile's agents did?**

**2 (page 3).** My short answer: Telekom should copy the slow work behind the number. Two reasons for this case: both sides publish numbers, and this software does something a chatbot doesn't. A customer writes, "Please move me to a cheaper tariff." A chatbot answers, "Here's how." An agent just does it. For that it needs keys: permission to see and change your account. **An agent doesn't just answer. It acts.**

**3 (page 5).** A correction to my own proposal. I used the forty percent from Telekom's press release. The release says AI agents handled that share of contacts. I couldn't find which customers it counted. T-Mobile's own slide gives a figure for contract customers: thirty-two percent of their contacts started with software. I couldn't find a figure for how many of those the software finished. These are company figures. **About one in three contacts from contract customers started with software.**

**4 (page 8).** The second number: at the end of 2021, there were about seven contacts a year for each customer account. By the middle of this year, about three. Less than half. But the agents were only announced in September 2024, and I found nothing published in between. **Nobody outside can tell how much was the agents.**

**5 (page 10).** My father is a civil engineer, and this picture is his. In America almost every new family home has a timber frame: the wall is hollow, so a new cable drops down inside it. In Germany it's mostly masonry, brick and block, built to stand, so a new cable means opening the wall. You can screw a duct onto the wall: quick, and the wall stays as it was. Or you cut a channel, lay the cable and plaster it over. That's slow and dusty, and afterwards the cable is part of the wall. The cable is the agent. The wall is everything already there: old systems, rules, people. The duct is repainting. Cutting in is renovating. **The cable is the same. The wall decides the work.**

**6 (page 14).** Look at what T-Mobile did. Their head of IT described it to investors. Outside contractors used to work on the suppliers' systems. Now T-Mobile's own engineers work on T-Mobile's own systems. They built one customer record, and the agents read from it. T-Mobile also reports that almost eight in ten chats never reached a person in mid-2026. That tells us where the chats ended. I'd still want to see how many customers got what they came for. **T-Mobile rebuilt alongside its agents.**

**7 (page 17).** Telekom isn't there yet. Across its European countries, a customer can prove who they are in fourteen different ways: fourteen sockets, and an agent would need a plug for each. Telekom wants three, and its own slide says why. **Simplify before automating.**

**8 (page 19).** From the street, repainting looks the same for a while, and it's cheaper. But if nothing changes behind the agent, the saving usually comes from the staff. By February 2024 Klarna had stopped hiring and said its assistant did the work of seven hundred people in customer service, by its own estimate. In 2025 it was hiring people again, and it kept the assistant. Commonwealth Bank went the same way, faster. It declared forty-five jobs redundant for a voice bot, and took that back within weeks. These are only two cases. Both cost the companies money and trust. **Both took part of it back.**

**9 (page 21).** The harder question is what the wall looks like in Germany. Three bricks matter: the staff, the rules and the know-how. The first is the staff. This May, Telekom and the union ver.di agreed: no dismissals for business reasons until the end of 2028. People still leave, but only by agreed routes: partial retirement, early retirement, severance. From 2021 to 2025 that was about four thousand full-time positions a year, across Telekom in Germany. Sixteen every working day. (pause) **So, first brick: no dismissals, but positions go.** Any saving has to fit into that. I think that helps more than it hurts: the people who stay are the first ones to train. That's what renovating means to me: the agent works with them, not instead of them.

**10 (page 22).** The second brick starts with a worry. The EU asked people about digital monitoring at work. In Germany seventy-nine percent saw it negatively. Only Sweden was higher. I'd want to know what my employer sees before I use the tool. **Nearly four in five Germans see monitoring at work as bad.**

**11 (page 23).** I looked for a public record showing staff had a say before T-Mobile launched. I couldn't find one. In Germany, if the tool can monitor staff, the works council has to agree before it runs. Telekom already has a framework from 2021. It rules out monitoring individual employees. Anonymous group results are allowed for groups of more than five. A system that fits those rules needs no separate agreement. That gives me something to work with. **The works council has a say before launch.**

**12 (page 26).** The third brick is know-how. The Federal Statistical Office went to the firms that had looked at these tools and didn't use them, and asked: why not? More than seven in ten said they were missing the knowledge. Legal uncertainty and data protection came after, each about six in ten. Most of those firms are much smaller than Telekom. But the order is clear. **Third brick: missing knowledge first. The law after.** You can buy a language model. You can screw it onto the wall. Without your own people who understand your systems, you can't cut it in.

**13 (page 28).** So how would I do it? Step one: before any agent, measure what one contact costs today, and how often customers come back. Then, with the agent: how many requests it finishes for good, and at what cost. That's the business measure from this course. If it doesn't get better, the agent is the wrong tool, and Telekom stops there. Step two: sign the works agreement while the system is still on paper. Step three: keep the option to switch suppliers. About half of German firms say they depend strongly on the US. Step four: train the staff before each new key. Measure teams, never single people, and watch the load on the people who stay: they get the hardest calls. **Measure first. Start beside the staff.**

**14 (page 31).** Step five. Fourteen ways of proving who you are become three, and the customer data becomes usable. That's the one customer record T-Mobile built. An agent can't act on an account it can't identify. This is the channel cut into the brick. It takes years, and it can fail. In 2025 T-Mobile gave up part of a new billing system. It wrote off two hundred seventy-eight million dollars, before tax. About a twelfth of that quarter's profit. **The biggest job is one customer record.**

**15 (page 34).** Take the customer who asked for a cheaper tariff. First the agent can show them their bill after they sign in. Then it can change the tariff, and they can undo it in one tap. A change to their bank details comes last. They confirm it in the app. The system enforces each key. At the stage gate, we measure errors and sign the annex. If cost per finished request fails to fall, or repeat contacts rise, we hold the next key. Staff and the works council sign off before the agent works alone. Then a named team runs it. There's always a way to a person. **Then the keys: read first, risky changes last.**

**16 (page 37).** Three things would prove me wrong. One: Telekom reports a German number like the thirty-two percent before its customer data is fixed. Then the record matters less than I think. Two: the service teams shrink faster than the agreed routes allow. Then the staff are leaving after all. Three: a works agreement comes months late. Then the law weighs more than I said. (last build) I can't prove the rebuild caused T-Mobile's result. I argue it from need: an agent can't act on an account it can't identify. People often say Germany is slow. Two economists, Hall and Soskice, argue the other way: economies like Germany's are better at step-by-step innovation than at radical innovation. The agent is the transformation. The way in is step by step, with the staff. I started out thinking Germany was the harder case, and I still do. **Telekom can't import the 32%. It can copy the slow work behind it.** Thank you.
