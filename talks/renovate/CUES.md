## Notes by slide

**1 (page 1).** Deutsche Telekom openly takes an American result as its benchmark. On 5 October it told its investors about T-Mobile: software agents there now do a big part of customer service, and Telekom wants the same in Europe by 2030. My position: born in the United States, studying in Germany, so I may see Germany as the harder case. That's why every claim has a source. **Can Telekom copy what T-Mobile's agents did?**

**2 (page 3).** Two reasons for this case: both sides publish numbers, and this software does something a chatbot doesn't. A customer writes, "Please move me to a cheaper tariff." A chatbot answers, "Here's how." An agent just does it. For that it needs keys: permission to see and change your account. In this course's terms, a transformation, not a change. **An agent doesn't just answer. It acts.**

**3 (page 6).** A correction to my own proposal. Telekom's release says AI agents now handle forty percent of customer contacts in the US; I used that number. In T-Mobile's own material I couldn't find it. Its slide counts something narrower: only contract customers, only contacts "served by an AI agent". Served means the customer meets the software first. Whether the software also finished the job, T-Mobile doesn't say. Company numbers, not audited. Still, one in three is a lot. **Telekom says forty percent. T-Mobile's own slide counts thirty-two percent.**

**4 (page 10).** The second number: at the end of 2021 a customer account got in touch about seven times a year. By the middle of this year, about three. Less than half. But the agents were only announced in September 2024, and nothing in between is published. **Nobody outside can tell how much was the agents.**

**5 (page 13).** My father is a civil engineer, and this picture is his. In America almost every new family home has a timber frame: the wall is hollow, so a new cable drops down inside it. In Germany it's mostly brick, built to stand, so a new cable means opening the wall. You can screw a duct onto the wall: quick, and the wall stays as it was. Or you cut a channel, lay the cable and plaster it over: slow, and the cable becomes part of the wall. The cable is the agent. The wall is what's already there: old systems, rules, people. The duct is repainting. Cutting into the wall is renovating. **The cable is the same. The wall decides the work.**

**6 (page 17).** T-Mobile didn't just repaint. Their head of IT described it to investors. Before, outside contractors worked on other companies' systems. Now T-Mobile's own engineers work on T-Mobile's own systems. They built one system that knows each customer, and the agents read from it. On that base, almost eight in ten chats never reached a person. A real result. **T-Mobile rebuilt alongside its agents.**

**7 (page 21).** Telekom isn't there yet. Across its European countries, a customer can prove who they are in fourteen different ways. Telekom wants three, and its own slide says why. **Simplify before automating.**

**8 (page 24).** From the street, repainting looks the same for a while, and it's cheaper. But if nothing changes behind the agent, the saving usually comes from the staff. In February 2024 Klarna had stopped hiring and said its assistant did the work of seven hundred service agents, by its own estimate. In 2025 it was hiring people again. Commonwealth Bank went the same way faster: forty-five roles declared redundant for a voice bot, the decision withdrawn within weeks. **Both had to walk it back.**

**9 (page 27).** The harder question is what the wall looks like in Germany. Three bricks matter. The first is the staff. This May, Telekom and ver.di agreed: no dismissals for business reasons until the end of 2028. People still go by agreed routes: partial retirement, early retirement, severance. From 2021 to 2025 that was about four thousand full-time positions a year, across Telekom in Germany. Sixteen every working day. Any saving has to fit into that. I think that helps more than it hurts: the people who stay are the first ones to train. **First brick: the staff stay.**

**10 (page 30).** The second brick starts with a worry. The EU asked people about digital monitoring at work. In Germany nearly four in five see it as a bad thing. On AI and jobs, Germans are no more pessimistic than Americans; on monitoring at work they are. Any new system here has to answer that first. **Nearly four in five Germans see monitoring at work as bad.**

**11 (page 32).** German law makes it answer before launch. At T-Mobile I found no say for the staff before launch. Here, two rules matter most. One: a system that can watch the staff needs the works council to agree before it runs. Two: since August, the AI Act says customers must be told when they talk to an AI system. And here Telekom is ahead. In 2021 it agreed the rules with its works council once: nobody gets watched as a person, and results are only looked at in groups of more than five, without names. A system inside those rules needs no new decision. **Second brick: here the checks come first.**

**12 (page 36).** The third brick is know-how. Destatis went to the firms that had looked at these tools and didn't use them, and asked: why not? More than seven in ten said they were missing the knowledge. Legal uncertainty and data protection came after, each about six in ten. Without your own people who understand your systems, you can buy a language model. You can screw it onto the wall. You can't cut it in. **Third brick: missing knowledge first. The law after.**

**13 (page 39).** So how would I do it? Step one: before any agent, measure what one contact costs today and how often customers come back; then, with the agent, how many requests it finishes for good and what it costs. That's the business measure from this course, not accuracy. If it doesn't get better, the agent is the wrong tool and Telekom stops there. Step two: sign the works agreement while the system is still on paper. Steps three and four: keep the option to switch suppliers, since about half of German firms with twenty or more staff depend strongly on American technology, and train the staff before each new key, measuring teams, never single people. **Measure first. Start beside the staff.**

**14 (page 43).** Step five. Fourteen ways of proving who you are become three, and the customer data becomes usable. An agent can't act on an account it can't identify. This is the channel cut into the brick. It takes years, and it can fail: in the third quarter of 2025 T-Mobile wrote off two hundred seventy-eight million dollars on a billing system it abandoned, about a tenth of its net income that quarter. **The biggest job is one customer record.**

**15 (page 46).** But reading doesn't have to wait. Step six: first the agent only reads, for customers already logged in: show a bill, check an order. Then actions you can undo. Last, the changes a fraudster could use, like a new bank account; the customer confirms those in the app. Each key is enforced by the systems, not by the model: the model can ask, the system decides. Steps seven and eight: each new key is an annex to the works agreement, staff and works council sign off before the agent works alone, and a named team runs it. As a process model, it's a project life cycle with a works-council gate and a stop point added. The order is my hypothesis. **Then the keys: read first, risky changes last.**

**16 (page 50).** If Telekom reports a German number like the thirty-two percent before its customer data is fixed, the record matters less than I think. If the service teams shrink faster than the agreed routes allow, the staff are leaving after all. If a works agreement comes months late, the law weighs more than I said. **Three things would prove me wrong.** (last build) People often say Germany is slow, and mean it as a weakness. Hall and Soskice argue the other way: economies like Germany's are better at step-by-step innovation than at radical innovation. Renovating is step-by-step work. **Telekom can't import the 32%. It can copy the slow work behind it.** Thank you.
